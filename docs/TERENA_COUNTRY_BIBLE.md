# Republic of Terena — Country Bible

## 1. At a glance

**Official name:** Republic of Terena  
**Demonym:** Terenan  
**Capital:** Valen  
**Population (2028):** 72,000,000  
**Administrative divisions:** 20 provinces + Federal District of Valen  
**National Assembly:** 420 seats, elected in 48 multi-member STV constituencies  
**President:** directly elected by national ranked-choice voting for a five-year term, maximum two terms  
**Default game start:** 1 January 2028  
**Incumbent president:** Mara Velic, Civic Reform Party, term-limited in 2028

Terena is a wealthy but unevenly developed industrial-maritime republic on the western shore of the Meridian Sea. Its geography is politically useful: a large western interior opens into a dense eastern coast, while the south is broken into peninsulas and islands. Five land neighbors surround it, including the much larger Vaskaran Federation to the west. No Terenan government can treat foreign policy as optional.

## 2. National identity

Terenan political culture is built around a contradiction. Citizens are proud of a strong national state created through centuries of political unification, but they are deeply suspicious of concentrated executive power. The phrase most associated with the modern constitution is **"two mandates, neither master"**: the president receives a direct national mandate to administer the state, while the Assembly receives a separate mandate to make and control the law.

The national flag, anthem and civic holidays date mostly from the democratic restoration rather than the old monarchy. The monarchy is viewed historically rather than as a live political project. Even most conservatives defend the republican constitution.

## 3. People, language and religion

About 78 percent of residents identify primarily as Terenan in census questions. The largest minorities are Vaskaran-origin communities in the western and northern border regions, Nigan communities in the south, and Graeven communities along the eastern border. Mixed identity is common in cities.

Terenan is the national administrative language. Vaskaran, Nigan and Graeven have protected regional status where local population thresholds are met. The constitution guarantees public-school language access without establishing ethnic electoral rolls.

Religion matters culturally more than institutionally. Roughly two-fifths of the population belongs to the historic Valenic Communion, about one-tenth to smaller Reformed churches, and a large plurality describes itself as nonreligious or only culturally religious. There is no established church and religious parties have been minor since the 1970s.

## 4. Physical and political geography

The map uses the exact Azgaar state-41 coastline, lakes and islands. The western third contains large low-density provinces and old industrial corridors. The center contains the Valen river-and-lake system. The eastern shore is heavily urbanized, with ports facing the Meridian Sea. The southern island belt has a distinct maritime and regionalist political culture.

## 5. Constitutional order

The current constitution was ratified in 1971 after the October Crisis exposed the dangers of the previous strong-presidency system.

### President

The president is both head of state and chief executive. The office is directly elected in a single nationwide ranked-choice election. A term lasts five years and no person may serve more than two elected terms. The president appoints ministers, executes laws, directs diplomacy, commands the armed forces subject to statutory war powers, nominates senior judges and can return legislation to the Assembly once.

The president **cannot dissolve the Assembly** and cannot call an early legislative election. Executive orders must be grounded in a statute or an enumerated constitutional power.

### National Assembly

The National Assembly has 420 members elected every four years by STV in 48 constituencies. District magnitude in the current map ranges from 7 to 10 seats and tracks population. The Assembly elects its Speaker but does not elect the president or a prime minister.

The Assembly controls taxation, appropriations, legislation, statutory delegations, investigations and treaty ratification. Committees may subpoena documents and compel ministerial testimony under judicially enforceable rules.

### Suspensive veto

The president may return a bill once with objections. If an absolute majority of the Assembly passes it again, it becomes law. There is no two-thirds ordinary veto override.

### Ministerial censure

Ministers enter office when appointed by the president. The Assembly does not confirm ordinary ministers. It may, however, remove an individual minister by a **55 percent censure vote**. The president then appoints the replacement. This lets the legislature check an executive team without turning the country into a parliamentary system.

### Budget continuity

The president submits a budget, but the Assembly may rewrite it. If a new budget is not enacted on time, the previous year's appropriations continue temporarily under inflation and statutory-index rules. Terena therefore does not have American-style government shutdowns.

### Regulation review

Major executive regulations are published to the Assembly. A majority may annul a regulation within 60 days if it concludes that the executive exceeded or misused delegated authority. The underlying statute remains in force.

### War powers

The president may respond immediately to an attack and may deploy forces for up to 30 days without new authorization. Continued offensive operations require an Assembly authorization. Intelligence operations and small evacuations operate under separate statutes and oversight rules.

### Emergency powers

A presidential emergency declaration expires after 14 days. The Assembly may extend it in 30-day increments and may terminate it at any time. The Constitutional Court receives expedited jurisdiction over emergency-power challenges.

### Recall and removal

Ordinary political disagreement cannot remove a president. Impeachment for serious constitutional or criminal misconduct requires a two-thirds Assembly vote and judgment by the Constitutional Court. Separately, three-fifths of the Assembly may refer a **national presidential recall** to voters; a majority vote removes the president and triggers a new presidential election.

### Constitutional Court

The Court has nine judges serving nonrenewable 12-year staggered terms. The president nominates; three-fifths of the Assembly must confirm. The Court reviews statutes, emergency actions, election disputes and conflicts between provincial and national authority.

## 6. Territorial government

Provinces have directly elected councils and governors. They control local transport, land use, primary and secondary education administration, public hospitals, policing frameworks and portions of taxation defined by national statute. The constitution guarantees provincial existence but permits boundary changes by national law plus consent of affected provincial voters.

Municipal councils and mayors form the main entry point for political careers. A player does not have to begin in national politics.

## 7. Electoral system

### Presidential election

All voters rank presidential candidates. Counting uses instant-runoff ranked-choice voting. Parties therefore care about second-choice acceptability, cross-endorsements and candidate relationships. A candidate can lead first preferences and still lose after transfers.

### Assembly election

The 48 STV constituencies elect 420 members. Voters rank individual candidates, including multiple candidates from the same party. This is intentional: intra-party caucuses compete at the ballot box as well as inside the Assembly.

Parties may nominate more than one candidate in a constituency. Candidate popularity, factional endorsements and local organization therefore affect which wing of a party wins seats even when total party support barely changes.

**Constituency boundaries are independent of province boundaries.** Terena's provinces are historical governments with very unequal populations, from small island provinces to Aurel and Southmark. The National Electoral Commission therefore draws population-balanced multi-member electoral regions without a requirement to stay inside one province. Many constituencies cross provincial lines. This avoids giving tiny provinces disproportionate Assembly representation while preserving provinces as meaningful governments for governors, services and local politics.

### Labour Party

Labour is the largest party and the historic vehicle of organized labor. Its **Social Democratic Caucus** favors a regulated market economy and expansive public services. The **Workers' Left** favors more public ownership, stronger wealth taxation and a less interventionist foreign policy. The **Workers' Bloc** is union-heavy and economically left but more culturally moderate. **Reform Labour** is younger, urban and pro-housing, pro-competition and strongly pro-Democratic Concord.

### National Union

The National Union is the main center-right party. The **One Nation Caucus** combines fiscal restraint with social insurance and national institutions. **Market Conservatives** favor lower taxes, deregulation and trade. **National Conservatives** emphasize immigration restriction, policing and military strength and compete with the People's Movement.

### Civic Reform Party

Civic Reform grew from the democratic constitutional movement. **Liberal Reform** is socially liberal, pro-electoral reform and internationalist. **Civic Moderates** emphasize competent administration, balanced budgets and cross-party bargaining. President Mara Velic belongs to the latter wing.

### Green Alliance

The Greens combine climate policy, civil liberties, transit, housing reform and social progressivism. They are strongest among younger urban voters and in Solenne.

### Regional League

The League is a federation of provincial and agrarian organizations. It supports decentralization, farm cooperatives, fisheries, local infrastructure and stronger provincial fiscal autonomy. It is not a separatist party.

### People's Movement

The Movement combines protectionism, anti-elite rhetoric, stricter migration policy, skepticism of major institutions and opposition to parts of the Democratic Concord agenda. Its voters include former industrial Labour voters and former National Union voters.

## 9. Party nominations

Nomination rules are intentionally different by party. Labour uses a ranked member ballot with union-affiliated delegates contributing a minority share to the presidential nomination. National Union uses a closed member RCV after a parliamentary-caucus endorsement threshold. Civic Reform uses an open registered-supporter RCV. The Greens use a member convention with transferable votes. Regional League uses weighted provincial delegates. People's Movement uses a direct member ballot but requires candidates to win endorsements from several provincial organizations.

These rules should be represented mechanically rather than reduced to one generic primary system.

## 10. Full political history of Terena

### 900–1228: basin principalities
The territory of modern Terena was divided among river principalities, coastal trading cities and southern island lordships. Valen began as a fortified market at the junction of river routes rather than as the largest settlement. Maritime cities in modern Shorren and Solenne were wealthier for long periods.

### 1228: League of Valen
Six inland and coastal rulers formed the League of Valen for mutual defense and common toll rules. The League created a permanent council and common commercial court. Later constitutional lawyers would romanticize it as the first Terenan representative institution, although ordinary people had no political rights.

### 1452: Crown of Terena
A dynastic settlement united most of the northern and central territories under the Crown of Terena. Southern provinces remained semi-autonomous. The monarchy standardized currency and law but left provincial estates intact.

### 1580–1690: maritime expansion
Eastern ports expanded dramatically as Meridian commerce grew. The crown built a navy, established fortified trading stations abroad and fought repeated short wars over straits and customs rights. Merchant wealth made the eastern cities politically distinct from the agrarian interior.

### 1711: Act of Union
A succession crisis ended with the Act of Union, which incorporated the southern provinces and created a national treasury. Provincial elites accepted centralization in exchange for guaranteed estates and tax bargaining. The modern provincial map still reflects several of these old jurisdictions.

### 1784–1787: Charter Revolt
Tax increases imposed without provincial consultation triggered coordinated refusal by merchants, guilds and several provincial estates. The monarchy survived but accepted the **Charter of Valen**, creating an elected lower chamber with limited property suffrage. The principle that taxation required representation became part of national political culture.

### 1820–1860: industrial revolution
Railways linked the Darsen and Aurel industrial belts to the eastern ports. Coal, steel, machinery and shipbuilding transformed the economy. Working-class mutual-aid societies became legal in 1839 and unions in 1857. Mass urbanization created lasting regional differences between industrial western cities, commercial eastern ports and rural Southmark.

### 1868: constitutional settlement
After demonstrations and a military refusal to fire on crowds, the crown accepted responsible government. The monarch retained appointment and foreign-policy powers, but elected ministries became normal. The franchise expanded in 1872 and again in 1891.

### 1896: universal adult suffrage movement
A coalition of labor, liberal and provincial reform organizations won universal male suffrage and a large expansion of women's municipal voting rights. Full equal national suffrage followed in 1919.

### 1909–1916: First Continental War
Terena entered the war in 1911 after attacks on shipping and a Vaskaran border incursion. Casualties, rationing and inflation destroyed confidence in the monarchy and traditional parties. Veterans' organizations and unions demanded a written republican constitution.

### 1917: First Republic
King Marek IV abdicated rather than order the army against mass demonstrations. A constituent assembly established the First Republic with a powerful elected president and a weak bicameral legislature. The constitution was democratic but dangerously dependent on informal restraint by the presidency.

### 1920s: mass-party democracy
Labour, Liberal Republican and Conservative parties developed nationwide organizations. Public pensions and workplace insurance expanded. The country also accumulated foreign debt and speculative bank exposure.

### 1929–1933: depression and constitutional collapse
The global Credit Panic caused bank failures and industrial unemployment. Coalition governments collapsed repeatedly. President Adrian Varek used emergency articles intended for wartime finance to govern around the legislature. Courts initially tolerated the measures as temporary.

### 1934–1942: National Directorate
Varek's supporters transformed emergency government into the authoritarian National Directorate. Opposition newspapers were restricted, unions were placed under state supervision and presidential decrees replaced ordinary law in many areas. Elections continued locally but national competition was hollowed out. This period is the single most important negative reference point in modern Terenan constitutional debate.

### 1938–1943: war and resistance
The Directorate aligned economically with Vaskara at the beginning of the Second Continental War. It did not initially enter the war. As Vaskaran forces expanded and Terenan soldiers were deployed to "secure" border zones, strikes and underground opposition grew. A failed 1941 crackdown split the officer corps. In 1942 a coalition of legislators, judges, union leaders and military commanders forced Varek from office. Terena formally joined the anti-Vaskaran coalition in 1943.

### 1946: Second Republic
A postwar constitution restored elections but retained a strong presidency because wartime leaders wanted a decisive executive. The legislature was made unicameral, however, because the prewar upper chamber was blamed for elite obstruction. The new National Assembly had broader social representation and proportional elections.

### 1948–1965: reconstruction and social compact
Labour-led and centrist governments created universal health insurance, expanded pensions, built public housing and nationalized the rail grid. Conservative governments later preserved most of the settlement while encouraging private industry. Union density peaked above 40 percent.

### 1966–1970: October Crisis
A sequence of bombings, separatist violence in a small border movement and Vaskaran military pressure led President Karel Dravic to declare a national emergency. Dravic extended emergency orders repeatedly, censored some publications and attempted to bypass Assembly appropriations. In October 1969 police violence against a mass protest produced a national strike. The Constitutional Court ruled parts of the emergency program unlawful, but its authority under the 1946 constitution was ambiguous.

Dravic resigned in 1970 after losing military and party support. Unlike the 1930s collapse, the state remained democratic. Political leaders concluded that personal restraint was not enough and called a constitutional convention.

### 1971: Dual-Mandate Constitution
The convention created the present system. The president remained directly elected but lost dissolution power and received only a suspensive veto. The Assembly gained control over money, investigations, ministerial censure and statutory regulation. Emergency powers received automatic expiration. A stronger Constitutional Court was created. Presidential elections adopted national ranked-choice voting; Assembly elections adopted STV.

### 1974: first elections under the new order
The first full election produced divided government. Rather than causing collapse, the institutions forced bargaining. This experience gave the constitution legitimacy. Terena joined the Democratic Concord the same year after an Assembly ratification vote.

### 1978–1988: Labor Compact era
Center-left governments strengthened collective bargaining, created sectoral wage councils and expanded technical education. Productivity grew rapidly, but older heavy-industry regions became dependent on protected firms.

### 1989–2000: market reform
A Civic-Conservative reform coalition reduced tariffs, privatized several competitive state enterprises and created an independent central bank. Labour later accepted most of the framework while preserving universal welfare programs. Eastern ports and Valen prospered; Caldria and parts of Aurel experienced industrial decline.

### 2001–2007: confident middle-power period
Terena participated in Concord peacekeeping, expanded university enrollment and became a major logistics and advanced-machinery exporter. Politics appeared increasingly centrist.

### 2008–2012: financial crisis and party fragmentation
A housing and bank shock pushed unemployment sharply higher. A bank rescue passed with Labour, Civic and National Union votes and became deeply unpopular. Anti-austerity movements strengthened Labour's left; regional fiscal disputes revived the Regional League; anti-establishment right-populists consolidated into the People's Movement.

### 2014–2019: Vaskaran pressure
Vaskara increased military exercises near the border, funded sympathetic media networks and used energy contracts as leverage. Terena responded by diversifying energy imports, expanding cyber defense and increasing defense spending. The issue split Labour and strengthened the pro-Concord wing of Civic Reform.

### 2018: Mara Velic elected president
Civic Reform candidate Mara Velic won after transfers from Green and moderate Labour voters. Her first term focused on ethics rules, infrastructure and Concord coordination.

### 2020–2022: pandemic and recovery
Emergency public-health rules became the first major stress test of the 1971 emergency provisions. The Assembly extended emergency authority in short increments, and the Court struck down two executive regulations while leaving the main health framework intact. The system's ability to fight intensely without collapsing increased public confidence in the constitution.

### 2023: Velic reelected
Velic defeated National Union candidate Tomas Kalen on the final RCV round. Labour's candidate finished third but Labour voters broke heavily toward Velic after Kalen made an electoral pact with the People's Movement in several provinces.

### 2024–2026: inflation, energy shock and divided government
Vaskaran energy restrictions and shipping disruption drove inflation. The government introduced subsidies and accelerated nuclear, renewable and grid investment. In the 2026 Assembly election, Labour became the largest party while Civic Reform lost seats. Labour, Civic Reform and Greens elected Daria Soren Speaker but did not form a permanent governing coalition because the president is independently elected.

### 2028: open presidential succession
Velic is term-limited. No party controls the Assembly. Labour leads on economic trust, National Union leads on crime and immigration, Civic Reform leads on institutional competence, and Green candidates have unusually strong second-choice support. Vaskaran relations remain tense. The default game therefore begins with a competitive national election and a legislature where issue-by-issue coalitions are normal.

## 11. Economy

Terena is an advanced mixed economy. Major sectors include machinery, shipbuilding, logistics, finance, pharmaceuticals, food processing, energy technology and software. The west and north contain more legacy manufacturing; the east contains finance and export services; the south remains more agricultural and maritime.

Unions represent about 27 percent of workers nationally and much more in transport, manufacturing and the public sector. Sectoral bargaining covers more workers than direct union membership. Public ownership remains significant in rail infrastructure, the grid and a minority of energy generation, while most consumer and manufacturing firms are private.

The 2028 policy arguments concern housing supply, energy costs, productivity, industrial subsidies, taxation of high incomes and wealth, the future of sectoral bargaining and whether strategic industries should receive protection from Vaskaran or Elzesh competition.

## 12. Media and civil society

The **Terenan Public Service (TPS)** is an independent public broadcaster funded by a multi-year appropriation formula. Major private outlets include the centrist *Valen Record*, center-left *Workers' Daily*, conservative *National Ledger*, market-liberal *Exchange*, green-leaning *Coast & City*, and populist digital network *Direct Terena*.

Important organizations include the Terenan Confederation of Labor, Federation of Manufacturers, National Farmers Cooperative, Small Business League, Civil Liberties Forum, Climate Action Network, Veterans Federation and several provincial associations. Organizations maintain endorsements, issue ratings, lobbying budgets and relationships with individual politicians.

## 13. Foreign relations

**Vaskaran Federation:** principal security competitor and largest difficult trading partner. Relations begin at -40. Border communities, energy and industrial supply chains prevent simple decoupling.

**Graeven Republic:** closest formal neighbor ally, +75. The two states share intelligence, infrastructure and a defense-planning framework.

**Kingdom of Nigis:** close southern security and maritime partner, +55. Fisheries and labor mobility still generate disputes.

**Republic of Bharesh:** generally friendly, +30, but agricultural trade and water management produce recurring arguments.

**Kingdom of Krigh:** pragmatic relationship, +20. Conservative governments in Krigh sometimes distance themselves from the Democratic Concord, but defense channels remain open.

**Soburish Federation:** primary extra-regional security partner, +65. Terena values the alliance but resists automatic alignment with every Soburish intervention.

## 14. Defense and security

Terena maintains a professional military with a small reserve system, a capable air force, a navy built around frigates, submarines and coastal defense, and a large cyber command. It does not possess nuclear weapons. Democratic Concord nuclear deterrence is extended through allied guarantees.

The military is institutionally wary of domestic politics because of its role in the 1942 and 1970 regime transitions. Active-duty officers are prohibited from party activity. Parliamentary committees have broad classified oversight powers.

## 15. Starting political environment and game hooks

The default start is deliberately unsettled without being an automatic crisis. The presidential succession is open. The Assembly must pass a 2028 budget. A major rail modernization bill is stalled between Labour, Civic Reform and Regional League negotiators. National Union is deciding how far to cooperate with the People's Movement. The Greens are debating whether to prioritize a carbon price or housing/transit legislation. Vaskara is conducting a large military exercise near the border. Several high-profile Constitutional Court terms will expire during the next presidential term.

None of these outcomes should be scripted. They establish incentives, relationships and pending decisions that the simulation can resolve differently in each save.
